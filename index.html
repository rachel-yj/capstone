Generative AI in marketing, and what audiences really think

The Authenticity Gap

Brands are racing to take advantage of generative AI, but audiences aren’t sold. This gap in expectations is where trust gets won or lost.

By Rachel Li · Rutgers University · Digital Communication, Information & Media Capstone

  • 83% of ad executives say their company has used AI to make creative, up from 60% in 2024. IAB
  • 50% of U.S. consumers would rather buy from brands that avoid generative AI in their ads and content. Gartner
  • −31.5% fewer clicks, at the largest drop measured, when ads were labeled as AI-generated in a field test. Lee et al.

Where Do You Stand?

Four quick questions to test how your instincts compare with the nation.

Before getting into the research, take a minute to check your own reactions to AI in advertising. Each answer shows how people across the country answered the same question.

reader_poll.exe

Think back on the last ad you scrolled past. These days, somewhere between brainstorming and publishing, generative AI was probably involved in its creation. Most advertising executives say their company has used AI to make creative, and fewer than half say they always tell audiences when they do (Williamson et al., 2026).

But how does the audience feel about it? To answer this guiding question, we look at national surveys, industry studies, controlled experiments, and the comment sections of the campaigns that went wrong.

The short answer is that, rather than the usage of AI itself, audiences care more about whether they feel misled, and the industry itself is underestimating how much they care. The six chapters below follow that gap from boardroom to comment section to the data centers that power these campaigns.

CHAPTER 01 / 06

The Rush

Why marketers adopted generative AI so quickly.

For marketers, the case for generative AI comes down to speed and cost, and the tools are now part of almost every media plan. Advertisers who use AI report using it for social media ads (85%), display (73%), TV (56%) and audio (42%) (Williamson et al., 2026).

The promise of lower costs explains a lot about why AI has spread so fast. In two years, cost efficiency went from a minor perk to the main reason executives give for using AI, while fewer named creative innovation as a benefit. Brands are increasingly putting out AI content because it is cheaper, not because the result is better.

Marketers are also more enthusiastic about AI than the people they're marketing to. Fewer than half of consumers (48%) say they like seeing AI-generated images in ads, and most (57%) worry about fake or misleading AI advertising (Bubani & Jones, 2026). Kantar's summary puts it simply: marketers see AI as a practical assistant, while consumers see a potentially untrustworthy creative engine.

CHAPTER 02 / 06

The Gap

Executives think young consumers like AI ads. Most of them don't.

Before you read on, take a guess. The Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB) asked U.S. advertising executives how they think Gen Z and millennial consumers feel about AI-generated ads. Then it asked those consumers directly.

The gap is growing. Two years earlier, it was 32 points; now it is 37 (Williamson et al., 2026). As young consumers see more AI ads, they're making up their minds, and more of them are thinking negatively. In two years, the share with no opinion fell from 34% to 25%, while negative sentiment rose 12 points.

Executives and consumers don't even describe the same brand the same way.

Executives see AI as proof that a brand is modern. Consumers are twice as likely to read it as a sign that a brand is cutting corners or trying to trick them. And many brands assume Gen Z is the most comfortable with this new technology, but Gen Z is showing the most skepticism.

Gen Z's negativity nearly doubled in two years, while millennials barely moved. Gen Z respondents are also far more likely than millennials to call brands that use AI ads inauthentic (30% vs. 13%), disconnected (26% vs. 8%) and unethical (24% vs. 8%) (Williamson et al., 2026). For brands looking to market to the next generation of buyers, that is the wrong direction.

The wariness isn't limited to young people. Pew Research Center asked 5,023 U.S. adults how they feel about AI's growing role in daily life and what it will do to people's ability to think creatively.

Concern is the default, and it is rising: in 2021, 37% of Americans felt more concerned than excited (Kennedy et al., 2025). And for an industry that sells creativity, the second finding is crucial. Far more Americans expect AI to weaken creative thinking than to strengthen it, so an ad that announces it was machine-made starts at a disadvantage.

That wariness reaches purchasing decisions. Gartner found that 68% of U.S. consumers frequently wonder whether the content they see is real, and its analysts now frame AI as a question of trust (Gartner, 2026).

“Marketers should treat GenAI as a trust decision as much as a technology decision.”

Emily Weiss, Senior Principal Analyst, Gartner (Gartner, 2026)

CHAPTER 03 / 06

The Paradox

AI ads can win the click and still lose the audience.

If consumers distrust AI ads, why do brands keep making them? Partly because, by some measures, they work.

Researchers at NYU Stern and Emory University ran test display ads on the Google Display Network: some made by human experts, some made by humans and edited with generative AI, and some generated entirely by AI. Then they watched what happened when the AI ads were properly disclosed (Lee et al., 2025). Try it below.

Fully AI-generated ads won the most clicks, but only while viewers didn't know. Ads that AI merely edited didn't beat the human originals at all. A single label erased the advantage and then some.

A NielsenIQ (NIQ) study found a different kind of tension. In a neuroscience study where more than 2,000 people watched AI-generated ads of varying quality, viewers could often tell intuitively that an ad was made with AI. They described those ads as more “annoying,” “boring” and “confusing” than traditional ads, and NIQ warns that this can drag down perceptions of the brand itself (NielsenIQ, 2024).

Even the words “artificial intelligence” carry a cost. In experiments with more than 1,000 U.S. adults across eight product and service categories, adding the term to an otherwise identical product description lowered purchase intent. The researchers traced the drop to lower emotional trust, and the effect was strongest for high-risk purchases like expensive electronics (Cicek et al., 2025).

THE SWITCH

Recognition flips the reaction. AI content can win attention when it looks polished and passes as human-made. Once people spot it or are told about it, the response turns.

The disclosure dilemma

Brands are thus left in a bind. Audiences want to know: as shown in our opening poll, most believe brands should disclose AI, and many doubt their ability to detect it. Getty Images found that nearly 90% of consumers want to know whether an image was made with AI (Getty Images, n.d.). But if labels cost clicks, is disclosure worth it?

The click data and survey data point in different directions. A label might cost initial clicks, but most young consumers say it wouldn’t stop them from purchasing. And ultimately, being caught hiding AI usage costs trust that lasts longer than any advertising campaign.

CHAPTER 04 / 06

The Backlash

Six campaigns, two years, one pattern.

The research is easiest to see in the campaigns that went wrong, and in the brands that learned from them. The biggest backlashes share a pattern: AI showed up where audiences expected authenticity most: warm moments, craftsmanship and real people.

BacklashCounter-move

case_01_coca-cola.txtBACKLASH
Nov 2024 & Nov 2025

Coca-Cola

“Holidays Are Coming,” remade with generative AI

Coca-Cola remade its classic 1995 holiday ad with generative AI and faced significant backlash. The company came back with AI again the next year, this time steering clear of close-ups of AI-generated humans (Aliieva, 2025).

A team of researchers analyzed 7,822 YouTube and Reddit comments on the remake and found the backlash ran on three levels at once (Heimstad et al., 2027):

  1. ContentVisual realism, execution quality and fit with the moment
  2. PsychologicalLost authenticity, creativity and human connection
  3. Socio-ethicalSuspected corporate greed, replaced workers and broader ethics

LESSON Backlash isn't only about AI looking worse. It's about what choosing AI says about a brand's values.

case_02_guess.txtBACKLASH
Aug 2025

Guess × Vogue

AI-generated models in a print ad

A Guess ad in Vogue's August 2025 issue featured two AI-generated models created by the London agency Seraphinne Vallora, and TikTok erupted. Creators objected to a fashion brand using models who don't exist, and critics said the images reinforced unrealistic beauty standards. One fashion creator summed it up: “This doesn't make me want to buy anything” (McCarthy, 2025).

LESSON Synthetic people are the fastest way to lose an audience, especially in categories built on real bodies.

case_03_aerie.txtCOUNTER-MOVE
Oct 2025

Aerie

A public promise of “Real people only”

Aerie went the other way. On October 9, 2025, it posted a pledge on Instagram: “No AI-generated bodies or people. Real people only.” The post drew more than 40,000 likes and became the brand's most popular post in a year, and engagement on its Instagram posts grew by about 75% between October 6 and 19 (Hart, 2025).

The brand framed the pledge as an extension of its 2014 promise to stop retouching photos. In 2026 it followed up with a campaign starring Pamela Anderson, shown here.

LESSON “Made by people” is becoming a selling point in its own right.

case_04_valentino.txtBACKLASH
Dec 2025

Valentino

An AI-generated handbag campaign

The luxury house promoted its DeVain handbag with AI-generated visuals on Instagram. The post was clearly labeled as AI, and commenters still called it “cheap,” “lazy” and “disturbing.” Anne-Liese Prem of the creative agency Loop told the BBC that Valentino showed “the right instinct” by being upfront, but that the backlash revealed “a deeper cultural tension” (McMahon, 2025).

LESSON Disclosure isn't a shield when craftsmanship is the product.

case_05_mcdonalds.txtPULLED
Dec 2025

McDonald's Netherlands

“The most terrible time of the year”

McDonald's Netherlands released a 45-second Christmas ad built entirely from AI-generated clips of holiday mishaps, ending with the line “hide out in McDonald's till January's here.” Viewers objected to both the AI and the cynical tone, and the company removed the ad within days (Aliieva, 2025).

LESSON Emotional moments raise the stakes. The holidays are the worst place to look automated.

case_06_etsy.txtCOUNTER-MOVE
Oct 2026

Etsy

New rules for AI in product listings

Etsy now requires most listings to include at least one unedited photo of the real product against a real background. Patterns, recipes and instructions can no longer be AI-generated, bot-generated listings are banned, and buyers may get refunds when an item doesn't match a listing that broke the new image rules. At the same time, Etsy is investing more in AI behind the scenes to improve search and personalization (James, 2026).

LESSON Use AI to help people find things, not to change what they think they're buying.

WHERE BACKLASH HITS HARDEST

  • Holidays and emotional moments
  • Synthetic people and bodies
  • Luxury, fashion and handmade goods

WHERE AI IS TOLERATED

  • Behind the scenes: search, matching, personalization
  • Clearly labeled, well-made work
  • Brands that keep real people visible

CHAPTER 05 / 06

The Footprint

The energy cost that never makes it into the campaign brief.

Every AI-generated image and video runs on data centers whose demand for electricity is growing fast. MIT Technology Review measured the energy behind individual AI requests, and marketing is moving quickly into the most energy-hungry format of all: video.

Because the scale is logarithmic, the bars understate the jump. One AI video uses roughly 770 times the energy of a high-quality image and about 30,000 times a short text answer (O'Donnell & Crownhart, 2025). A campaign that generates and tests many video variations multiplies that cost with every version.

One request is small, but billions come with a heavy cost. Driven largely by AI, data centers are taking a growing share of the power grid, and the electricity they use is dirtier than average, so AI's footprint grows with every brand that ramps up its usage (O'Donnell & Crownhart, 2025; IEA, 2025).

Sustainability is a perception, too

Consumers bring the same instincts to sustainability that they bring to authenticity. In experiments using everyday products like a backpack, a toothbrush, a reusable cup and a tote bag, people rated AI-designed items as less sustainable than identical items described as human-designed. Participants who prioritized sustainability in their decision-making overwhelmingly opted for the human-designed option, and in a field experiment, ads for AI-designed products earned lower click-through and engagement (Duffek & Biswas, 2026).

The researchers traced the gap to perceived “genuine care”: people associate sustainability with human intention, something people don’t expect from a machine. Proving that human designers stayed involved shrank the effect.

Green messaging made with AI has its own trap. Moderately green AI-generated content raised consumers' pro-environmental perceptions and purchase intent, but overly green content triggered skepticism about greenwashing and pushed purchase intent back down (Zhou & Jiang, 2025).

THE THREAD

Whether the question is authenticity or sustainability, audiences are looking for the same thing: evidence that a person cared.

CHAPTER 06 / 06

The Playbook

What the research suggests brands should do.

The research never indicated that brands should stop using generative AI, but demonstrated that the industry has been reading the wrong signal. Clicks and cost savings measure why brands choose AI, but trust measures how consumers see the brand. Five lessons to take away:

  • Disclose plainly

    Most consumers want to know, and hiding AI costs more trust than labeling it. Put the label where people will actually see it.

    Cint, 2026 · Getty Images, n.d. · Williamson et al., 2026

  • Keep people in the picture

    Synthetic people draw the strongest backlash. Showing real people, and human oversight of AI work, rebuilds the sense of care audiences look for.

    McCarthy, 2025 · Hart, 2025 · Duffek & Biswas, 2026

  • Match the tool to the moment

    Use AI where it helps people find and choose. Keep it away from holidays, emotional stories and craft-driven products, where it reads as a shortcut.

    James, 2026 · Aliieva, 2025 · McMahon, 2025

  • Treat quality as a trust signal

    Low-quality AI visuals distract from the message and can drag down the whole brand. If it looks automated, audiences assume nobody cared.

    NielsenIQ, 2024 · Heimstad et al., 2027

  • Count the footprint

    AI video carries a real energy cost. Weigh it before generating at scale, and don't let AI overstate green claims that audiences will read as greenwashing.

    O'Donnell & Crownhart, 2025 · Zhou & Jiang, 2025

The authenticity gap isn't fixed. Exposure is rising, younger consumers are forming their opinions now, and platforms like Etsy are still writing their rules. The brands that close the gap will be the ones that treat their audience's trust as part of the creative brief.
